Prayer times are a calculation, not a ruling

SalahNow computes prayer times on your phone from your position, the date, and a calculation method you choose. It contacts no server and receives no timetable from anyone.

That calculation is astronomy, and astronomy is not the whole of the matter. Methods differ, and reasonably: scholars disagree about the twilight angles for Fajr and Isha, about the shadow ratio that begins Asr, and about what to do at latitudes where the sun does not set. The app offers the established methods and applies the one you pick. It does not decide between them, and it is not a religious authority.

Where the app and your local mosque disagree, follow your mosque. If a time looks wrong, check the method, the Asr rule, the high-latitude rule and any offsets in Settings before relying on it.

The Qibla is as good as your phone's compass

The Qibla bearing is exact arithmetic from your position to the Kaaba. Pointing it at the world is not: that depends on your phone's magnetometer, which is affected by metal, magnets, cases, speakers and vehicles. The app shows you how much it trusts the reading and asks you to calibrate when it does not. Treat a compass bearing as a good indication, not a survey.

Notifications may not arrive, and often that is not the app

Prayer alerts are scheduled with Android's alarm services and delivered by your phone. Several things outside the app can stop them: notification permission withdrawn, exact alarms disallowed, the phone in a silent or focus mode, or — most commonly — a manufacturer's battery manager closing the app after you swipe it away. Several popular makes ship one of these, and no application programming interface lets an app override it.

The app tells you when it can detect this and points you at the setting. Do not rely on SalahNow as your only means of knowing a prayer time has come.
